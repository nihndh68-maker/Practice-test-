The Changing Design of Museums

For much of their history, museums were designed to impose order on objects. Early European ‘cabinets of curiosities’ often brought together natural specimens, antiquities, scientific instruments and works of art. Their architectural setting was usually secondary to the act of collecting itself: rooms, cases and catalogues helped present rare objects as evidence of knowledge and status. By the nineteenth century, many museum buildings had become formal civic institutions, with classical façades, grand staircases and symmetrical galleries suggesting that culture was stable, authoritative and separate from everyday urban life.

This model has not disappeared, but it has been repeatedly challenged. Modern museum design increasingly treats the building not only as a container for collections, but also as public space and sometimes an exhibit in its own right. One important shift has been from fixed display to flexible use. Curators now expect galleries to accommodate changing exhibitions, digital installations and educational activities. As a result, architects must balance conflicting demands: galleries need environmental control and protection from excessive light, while visitors often expect openness, social space and visual connection with the city.

The Centre Pompidou in Paris, opened in 1977, made this conflict unusually visible. Designed by Renzo Piano and Richard Rogers, it placed much of its structure and services on the outside of the building. The brightly coloured pipes, ducts and escalators were not hidden as internal necessities. They became part of the museum’s public identity. This inside-out strategy created large, adaptable spaces within the building, while the external escalator turned movement into an urban performance. Just as significant was the broad plaza in front, which encouraged informal gathering. The museum therefore suggested that contemporary culture could be open, noisy and democratic.

A different turning point came with the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao, designed by Frank Gehry and opened in 1997. Its sweeping forms of titanium, glass and limestone on a prominent site next to the Nervión River, were widely seen as evidence that a museum could operate as a landmark. The building occupied a former industrial area and became closely associated with Bilbao’s wider regeneration. Yet this example also created a problem for later museum projects. The so-called ‘Bilbao effect’ encouraged cities to commission spectacular buildings in the hope of attracting tourists and investment, although architecture alone rarely produces such results. Bilbao worked partly because the museum was linked to a broader urban strategy.

Other museums have pursued transformation through reuse rather than spectacle. Tate Modern in London, opened in 2000, occupies the former Bankside Power Station, converted by Herzog & de Meuron. Instead of disguising the building’s industrial past, the architects retained its massive brick exterior, central chimney and Turbine Hall. This vast interior space changed expectations about what an art museum could contain, creating an environment where works too large for conventional galleries can be displayed. The project also showed how museums could conserve important reminders of an area’s past through the museum building itself.

More recently, museum design has paid closer attention to climate, locality and atmosphere. Louvre Abu Dhabi, which opened in 2017 and cost around $650 million to build, is arranged as a low-lying museum city beneath a broad patterned dome. The dome filters sunlight to create shifting patterns of light, while also contributing to shade and a comfortable microclimate. Its design mirrors regional forms such as shaded public spaces, but it also belongs to a global museum network. The building therefore illustrates a current tension: museums are expected to be internationally recognisable while also responding convincingly to local environmental and cultural conditions.

These examples point to several practical principles in contemporary museum planning. Circulation, the way people are directed around the building, is no longer just a way of moving from one room to another. It shapes how visitors understand a collection. Entrances, ramps and staircases can orient people, slow them down or encourage exploration. Flexibility is equally important, since exhibitions may require different lighting levels or wall arrangements. Museums must also provide spaces that do not depend on ticketed displays, including cafés, learning rooms and public foyers.

The changing design of museums is partly a response to changing expectations of culture itself. Visitors still come to see objects, but they also come to meet, rest, learn, photograph, debate and take part in events. This does not mean that architecture should compete with what’s on display. On the contrary, the most successful museum buildings tend to make an argument about how knowledge should be encountered. Some invite the city inside, while some preserve traces of an earlier industrial life. Others use light and climate as materials. What they share is the recognition that a museum is not a neutral box, but a carefully designed relationship between objects, people and place.

The World Beneath the Forest

When people walk through a forest, they usually notice what is above ground: trunks, branches, and leaves. Yet some of the most important activity in a forest takes place in darkness, beneath the soil. Here, tree roots grow among fine threads of fungi, forming living connections that may link one plant to another. This hidden system is sometimes called the ‘wood wide web’, a phrase that captures the public imagination but can make the science sound simpler than it really is.

The basic process begins with a partnership between trees and fungi. Most trees cannot absorb all the water and minerals they need directly from the soil with their roots alone. Fungi, however, are extremely good at reaching into tiny spaces between soil particles. Their thread-like structures, known as hyphae, spread through the ground and collect nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen. In return, trees provide the fungi with sugars produced through photosynthesis. This relationship, called a mycorrhizal association, is one of the foundations of forest life.

In many forests, the same fungal growth may connect with the roots of more than one plant. This creates what scientists call a common mycorrhizal network. Through such networks, substances can move from one plant to another, at least under some conditions. Experiments have suggested that carbon, nutrients and chemical signals may pass through these underground links. For example, a tree growing in shade might be able to access carbon from a larger neighbour that has better exposure to sunlight. Seedlings may also benefit from connections to older trees while they are still too small to compete effectively.

One reason the idea has become so popular is that it seems to show trees behaving almost like members of a community. Research has suggested that when a plant is attacked by insects, chemical warning signals may travel through the soil, causing nearby plants to strengthen their defences. These findings have encouraged a more cooperative view of forests. Instead of seeing trees only as rivals for light, water and space, many people now imagine forests as connected systems.

This view has also influenced thinking about forest management. If mature trees are important hubs in underground networks, then removing them may do more than simply create gaps in the canopy. It may also disturb relationships below ground that help younger trees establish themselves. A forest, from this perspective, is not just a collection of individual trees but a network of relationships among plants, fungi, bacteria, animals and soil.

However, the story is not as settled as popular accounts sometimes suggest. One problem is that it is difficult to study underground networks in natural forests. Researchers can trace the movement of substances in controlled experiments, but forests are far more complex than laboratory systems. Roots may touch directly, fungi may connect with several species at once, and nutrients may also move through soil water. As a result, it can be hard to prove exactly which pathway a substance has taken, or whether the receiving tree has gained a meaningful benefit.

Another difficulty is the language used to describe the process. Verbs such as ‘talk’, ‘share’ and ‘help’ can be misleading as they oversimplify the connection, though they are useful for communicating ideas vividly to the public. Trees do not send messages with conscious intention. What looks like generosity may sometimes be the result of fungal activity that benefits the fungus more than either tree. In other cases, resources may move simply because of differences in concentration, rather than because one tree is assisting another.

Recent scientific debate has therefore become more cautious. Some researchers argue that the evidence for large-scale, beneficial resource transfer between adult trees is weaker than many people assume. They do not deny that mycorrhizal networks exist, or that fungi are essential to forests. Their concern is with the stronger claim that trees routinely use these networks to support relatives, rescue weak neighbours or organise themselves like a social network.

A balanced conclusion may be that underground fungal networks are both real and easily romanticised. They clearly play a major role in helping trees obtain nutrients and survive in difficult environments. They may also allow signals and resources to move between plants in ways that affect forest growth. At the same time, the exact scale and importance of this movement are still being investigated. The forest beneath the forest is not a simple story of trees talking to each other. It is a living system of exchange, competition and dependence, and scientists are still learning how to interpret its messages.

Aquaculture: Benefits and risks

A For much of human history, fish came mainly from rivers, lakes and the open sea. That picture has changed dramatically. Aquaculture – the farming of fish, shellfish, seaweed and other aquatic organisms – now provides a major share of the seafood people eat. Its supporters often present it as a straightforward answer to two linked problems: rising demand for protein and declining wild fish stocks. That argument is persuasive, but only up to a point. Aquaculture can certainly reduce pressure on some wild fisheries, provide nutritious food and support coastal and rural economies. Yet it is not automatically sustainable simply because it takes place on farms rather than in the wild. The real question is not whether aquaculture is good or bad, but which forms of it deserve to expand, and under what conditions.

B The strongest case for aquaculture is that the world’s appetite for seafood cannot be met by wild fisheries alone. Many wild stocks are already heavily exploited, and even well-managed fisheries cannot keep increasing their catch indefinitely. Farming aquatic species offers a way to increase supply without sending more boats into already pressured waters. It can also provide relatively affordable protein, particularly in regions where fish is a central part of the diet and other animal protein is expensive. In this respect, dismissing aquaculture would be irresponsible. If governments want secure food systems, they cannot simply tell people to eat less seafood. They need production methods that are both scalable and carefully controlled. Aquaculture is therefore not a luxury industry or a marginal experiment. In many places, it is becoming part of basic food planning.

C Aquaculture can also bring economic benefits, especially where other sources of income are limited. Fish ponds, cages and shellfish farms may create work not only on farms themselves, but also in hatcheries, feed production, processing, transport and sales. For some small producers, aquaculture can diversify income and reduce dependence on uncertain wild catches. It may also keep more value within a local area if farmers, processors and sellers are connected through local markets. However, this optimistic picture needs qualification. Large, export-oriented farms may generate profit without doing much for local food security or fair employment. If licences, technology and market access are controlled by a few companies, local communities may carry the environmental costs while receiving few of the rewards. The social value of aquaculture therefore depends on ownership, labour conditions and regulation, not just output.

D The environmental record of aquaculture is mixed. Some forms are genuinely low impact. Shellfish and seaweed farming, for example, do not require manufactured feed in the same way that fish farming does, and they may even improve local water quality by absorbing nutrients. Farm structures can sometimes provide habitat for other marine life, while seaweed cultivation may help remove excess nitrogen from coastal waters. These examples matter because they show that aquaculture is not inherently damaging. Nevertheless, it is misleading to use them to defend all aquaculture equally. A well-sited mussel farm and an intensive salmon farm are not environmental equivalents. Any serious discussion must distinguish between species, location, stocking density and management practices. Without those distinctions, the word ‘aquaculture’ becomes too broad to be useful.

E The risks become clearer in intensive systems. Uneaten feed and fish waste can add excess nutrients to surrounding water, encouraging algal growth and reducing oxygen levels. Diseases and parasites may spread quickly when animals are crowded together, and escapes from farms can threaten wild populations through competition or interbreeding. Some farms also rely on antibiotics, chemicals or feed ingredients derived from wild-caught fish. This last point is especially important: a farm that depends heavily on fishmeal and fish oil may simply move pressure from one part of the ocean to another. These problems are not imaginary objections raised by opponents of development. They are predictable outcomes when production is expanded faster than monitoring and enforcement. Technology can reduce some risks, but it cannot compensate for poor siting, weak regulation or a business model that treats ecosystems as free disposal systems.

F The sensible position, then, is selective expansion rather than blanket enthusiasm. Aquaculture should grow where it replaces more damaging production, strengthens local food systems and operates within ecological limits. Policymakers should favour species that require fewer external inputs, farms that recycle nutrients, and systems whose benefits reach local communities. Certification schemes and clearer labelling can help, but they should not be used as a substitute for public regulation. Governments still need to set limits, inspect farms and make companies responsible for the damage they cause. They should be much more cautious about projects that promise cheap protein while transferring pollution, disease risk or habitat loss to the public. The future of aquaculture should not be decided by asking whether fish farming is better than fishing. It should be decided by asking a tougher question: which farms would still look like progress if their full environmental and social costs were honestly counted?
