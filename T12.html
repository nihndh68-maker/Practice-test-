What is the environmental impact of electric vehicles?

Electric vehicles (EVs) are often viewed as a cleaner alternative to petrol and diesel cars because they do not produce exhaust fumes. As a result, they can improve air quality in urban environments. However, to understand their true environmental impact, it is necessary to examine the entire lifecycle of EVs. This includes the manufacturing process, the way the vehicles operate during use, and what happens when they reach the end of their useful life. The key issue is therefore whether electric vehicles genuinely reduce environmental damage and how important they may become in the future.

The manufacture of electric vehicles can release higher carbon emissions than that of conventional petrol or diesel cars. On average, producing an electric vehicle generates about 8.8 tonnes of CO₂, compared with 5.6 tonnes for a traditional vehicle. This difference is mainly due to the production of the battery, which accounts for approximately 43% of total manufacturing emissions. As a result, researchers are developing alternative battery technologies, including sodium-ion batteries, which may reduce reliance on lithium in the future.

Lithium extraction itself can create additional environmental problems. For instance, mining operations in Chile’s Atacama Desert, one of the driest regions in the world, require extremely large quantities of water in order to obtain soluble lithium minerals. This process places considerable pressure on local water resources and agricultural land and can damage fragile ecosystems.

During everyday operation, electric vehicles produce fewer harmful pollutants than petrol or diesel vehicles. They do not release the toxic fumes and soot typically produced by combustion engines. In addition, electric vehicles are generally quieter, which helps to reduce noise pollution in cities.

However, electric vehicles are not free from environmental impacts. Electricity used to charge vehicles is often generated using fossil fuels, meaning that some emissions still occur indirectly. In addition, all traffic produces airborne particles through tyre friction and brake wear. These microscopic particles contribute to approximately 28% of the microplastics found in seas and oceans.

Because electric vehicles contain large batteries, they are usually about 25% heavier than comparable petrol or diesel cars. This additional weight increases tyre wear, which may raise the amount of particles that enter the environment. On the other hand, electric vehicles use electronic braking systems, which significantly reduce the amount of wear on brake pads, although other components such as brake discs are also subject to wear.

Once a vehicle reaches the end of its useful life, recycling is an important environmental concern. In the United Kingdom, current legislation requires that 95% of a vehicle’s total weight must be recycled. In practice, this means that cars are routinely dismantled so that reusable materials can be recovered.

However, recycling electric vehicle batteries remains more complicated. According to the EU Battery Directive, at least 50% of battery components must be recycled. Materials such as wiring and plastics are relatively easy to recover, but lithium cells themselves contain hazardous substances that must be handled carefully to avoid environmental contamination.

One possible solution is to repurpose batteries in second-life applications. Batteries that are no longer suitable for powering vehicles can still store electricity in systems such as solar energy storage units, extending their useful lifespan.

Despite their environmental advantages, adoption of EVs varies widely around the world. In the United States, ownership levels remain relatively low compared with Europe. Within the country, however, the state of California has significantly higher levels of electric vehicle use due to its environmental policies and higher incomes.

The Scandinavian countries currently lead the world in electric vehicle ownership. Norway has approximately 117 electric vehicles per 1,000 people, which is roughly twice the figure recorded in Iceland. Sweden has fewer electric vehicles, averaging about one third of Norway’s level, although this is still slightly higher than California.

Governments in many countries are introducing policies to encourage electric vehicle adoption. In France, low-income drivers can lease a car for €100 per month, while in the United States a tax credit of $7,500 is available to certain buyers.

Future regulations are expected to further accelerate the transition to electric vehicles. In the United Kingdom, new cars sold from 2030 must produce partial zero emissions, and by 2035 the UK and the European Union plan to ban the sale of new petrol and diesel vehicles entirely.

Researchers suggest that improvements in battery production will significantly reduce environmental impacts in the future. A study conducted along an Italian highway concluded that at least 50% of vehicles must be electric in order to significantly reduce traffic pollution. Even a smaller proportion, however, can improve air quality during periods of severe pollution.

Although electric vehicle ownership has increased steadily, they still represent far less than half of all vehicles in Europe. Nevertheless, governments are increasingly recognising their value as a tool to reduce pollution. Respiratory illnesses are rising in many cities, prompting authorities such as those in London to introduce controversial road charges for non-electric vehicles.

The Benin Bronzes

All ancient objects, especially valuable objects, carry their own unspoken history. This is as true of the ‘Benin Bronzes’, as it is of many other of the world’s treasures. The Benin Bronzes is a collective term used to describe thousands of works of art, made of bronze and a range of other materials, which were looted by British troops at the end of the 19th century from the historic West African kingdom of Benin, which is now largely incorporated into Nigeria.

The artefacts were once used to decorate the palace of the king, or Oba of Benin, in holy shrines and at other locations in the historic city of Benin. The capture and desecration of the town by the British was in retaliation for the deaths in 1897 of members of a peaceful, yet provocative, British trade mission. In the violent occupation, thousands of objects were seized as ‘spoils of war’ and later either given or sold to European museums and collectors or kept by the soldiers and their families. Currently, the British Museum has the largest share of the haul, though there are sizeable collections both in Germany and the United States.

Though the practice was widespread at the time, the term ‘spoils of war’ was only codified in law by the 1907 Hague Regulations. Still in effect today, these state that “an army of occupation can… take possession of cash, funds and… securities which are strictly the property of the State” and so are legally held, whereas “the forcible taking of private property” is considered ‘pillage’ and is illegal. Nevertheless, Nigeria has long requested the return of the Bronzes, calls that have been fuelled by the ‘Black Lives Matter’ movement, which is urging a revaluation of Europe’s colonial past.

However, some owners of the artworks have taken the issue into their own hands. Students at Jesus College, Cambridge campaigned against the morality of the institution’s ownership of a bronze cockerel, which was officially returned to Nigeria as “the right thing to do out of respect for the unique heritage and history of this artefact”. Similarly, and following similar pressure, the University of Aberdeen has handed back a bronze bust in line with its “values as an international, inclusive university”. Germany has signed a ‘memorandum of understanding’ that has transferred the ownership of over a thousand artworks to Nigeria. The position of the British Museum remains uncertain. The ‘British Museum Act 1963’ forbids its trustees to ‘de-accession’, or give back, items in its collection. However, the museum is willing to loan items to Nigeria and has set up an open-access online virtual exhibition of their collection.

The beauty and quality of the Benin Bronzes is now universally acknowledged. Yet, at first colonial powers refused to believe a civilisation from Africa could have produced such stunning works. There is a long history of metalworking in both West and Central Africa. It has recently been discovered that African metallurgists were targeted by British slave traders because it was thought their innovations could benefit Britain’s Industrial Revolution. The slave trade impacted on West African crafts in other ways, as the bronze used in the Benin sculptures was created from metal ingots used as payment to the Benin kingdom for the sale of fellow Africans. Indeed, African American campaigners have demanded a halt to repatriations as they would benefit the descendants of African slave traders.

If the Bronzes are returned to Africa, their final destination remains uncertain. The British Museum and other European donors are backing a plan to set up a Museum of West African Art in the Nigerian state of Edo. However, the outgoing president of Nigeria has claimed the current ruler of the Kingdom of Benin, Ewuare II, is the legitimate owner of the Bronzes. The Nigerian government is also planning a museum of national unity in the country’s capital of Abuja. It is unclear which plan will become reality should the Bronzes return to Africa.

All of this illustrates the difficulties in returning valuable artefacts and antiquities to their original owners, or those who consider they have a moral right of ownership. The 1907 Hague agreements have been subsequently built on, most notably by the 1954 Hague Convention, spurred on by looting carried out by Nazi Germany, which expressly protects cultural property during armed conflict. However, this does not apply retroactively. Cases of individual ownership remain fraught, and many remain unresolved over decades, as is the case of the restitution of examples of stolen Jewish-owned art confiscated during World War Two. Collective ownership of nationally important antiquities presents further difficulties and remains a moral, more than a legal question.

How will Artificial Intelligence change the arts?

A In 2023, a song called Heart on My Sleeve attracted millions of listeners online. It was not an original Drake release; rather, it was a new song that used artificial intelligence to imitate the voices of Drake and The Weeknd. The popularity of the track led to wider debate about the future of creative work. AI-generated images have won art competitions, and AI tools are increasingly being used in writing, music and film production. As these technologies develop, many people are asking whether artists, musicians and writers may one day face serious competition from computers.

B Some experts believe that human creators are unlikely to disappear. Music producer Fernando Garibay, for example, argues that music production still depends on many human skills that machines cannot fully replace. In his view, AI systems may imitate style, but they cannot reliably produce the emotional force that creates a strong connection with listeners. This idea is related to the well-known ‘Turing Test’, which suggests that a machine would succeed only if its output were indistinguishable from that of a human. Research has also suggested that people may respond differently to human-made and AI-generated visual art. Similarly, although the Writers Guild of America has accepted that AI can be used in some parts of film production, it has insisted on preserving the role and rights of human writers.

C At the same time, AI can support the creative process rather than replace it. In a study of one novelist’s writing process, researcher Lee Trenholm found that AI could help at moments of writer’s block, when ideas seem to run out. The system produced unexpected suggestions, including new plot directions and song lyrics for a character to sing. Even though the writer did not use the material directly, it helped renew their interest in the story. Individual composers are also using AI to make their work more efficient, by generating supporting elements such as backing orchestration, which saves time during production.

D The music industry is already making practical use of AI. Streaming platforms such as Spotify use algorithms to recommend songs and create personalised playlists. Record companies analyse listening trends in order to identify new opportunities and maintain market share. AI can also help track where music is being used online, which may allow artists to receive payment more effectively. However, the legal position remains uncertain in some areas. In the United States, copyright protection has been limited to material created by human beings, which means that some AI-generated works may not qualify for the same protection as human-created ones.

E Much media discussion of AI in the arts has focused on well-known singers, major galleries and successful film franchises. However, these represent only a small part of artistic employment. Many musicians, visual artists and writers depend on less visible work for their income, such as background music for video productions, commercial illustration and advertising copy. These routine forms of creative work may be especially vulnerable to automation. They are also important because they often provide training and experience for people who later move into more prestigious areas of the arts.

F Some commentators claim that AI will ‘democratise’ the arts by allowing many more people to produce music, images and writing at little or no cost. A high-end London art gallery recently had a show of AI-generated canvases, with some predicting an AI art ‘gold rush’ for first-timer, home-based artists. AI image generators can create detailed pictures from short written prompts. Supporters argue that such tools may open up creative opportunities to people who do not belong to traditional artistic circles. Others disagree. Critics argue that if computers do too much of the creative work, human engagement may decline and long-term creative development may actually be weakened rather than expanded.

G The rapid growth of AI has caused concern well beyond the arts. Governments, companies and professional communities are increasingly being urged to treat AI as a permanent feature of modern life and to respond accordingly. In the United Kingdom, for example, public investment has gone into research on trustworthy AI, and official bodies have been established to consider standards, opportunities and risks. Because the creative industries are often seen as important to national economic competitiveness, policymakers are now examining how AI can be used to encourage growth while also protecting human creativity and employment.
